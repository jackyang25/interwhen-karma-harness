We evaluated interwhen-style procedural input verification on a clinical
tool-calling benchmark and found that no pre-registered verifier
configuration significantly improved over the no-verifier baseline.
Under a tool-forcing system prompt, the verifier with upfront full-schema
extraction actively reduced accuracy by 9.0 percentage points
(McNemar $p \approx 10^{-15}$). A flag-composition diagnostic traced the
harm to extractor unreliability --- a single field accounted for nearly
a third of all verifier flags --- rather than to the verifier's
mechanically correct comparison. A follow-up condition with reactive
per-tool-call focused extraction partially supported the schema-size
hypothesis (the dominant-field flag share more than halved and the
intervention-row correct rate doubled) but did not significantly recover
accuracy. The architecture was, however, $\sim$57\% cheaper in total
tokens, making reactive a Pareto improvement on cost regardless of the
accuracy verdict. The practical implication is that verifier-guided
reasoning in clinical AI should be deployed only after the upstream
reference pipeline is independently validated on the deployment's
specific field set, since the verifier's correctness guarantee is no
stronger than its reference.
