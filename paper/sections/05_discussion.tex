\subsection{What the Results Show}
\label{sec:disc_summary}

Four findings, ordered by certainty.

\textbf{1. Procedural input verification with an LLM-extracted
reference did not improve accuracy.} In the pre-registered primary
family (E, D, C vs B), no condition was significant at Bonferroni
$\alpha = 0.0125$ (\cref{sec:results_primary}). The only significant
primary contrast was B vs A, which confirms tool access matters but
says nothing about verification. We emphasize the qualifier
``LLM-extracted'': because our reference itself is produced by Sonnet
(empirically $\sim$80\% accurate on Condition B as a sanity check),
this result cannot distinguish ``the verifier mechanism does not help''
from ``the verifier mechanism does not help \emph{when its reference
is imperfect}''. A condition with a ground-truth (hand-annotated)
reference would separate these and is the most important comparator
this study lacks (see \cref{sec:disc_limitations}).

\textbf{2. Under forced tool use, the verifier actively hurt by 9.0 pp}
(B$'$+E vs B$'$; $p \approx 10^{-15}$; 118 vs 22 discordant pairs;
\cref{sec:results_exploratory}). This is one of the largest exploratory
effects in the study and is in the wrong direction.

\textbf{3. The harm came jointly from the reference and the verifier's
comparison surface.} Flag composition is concentrated on \texttt{sex}
(32\% of all flags under upfront extraction), a structurally easy
field. This implicates the \emph{extractor} (Sonnet on a $\sim$500-field
flat schema) for populating fields the active tool call did not
require. It \emph{also} implicates the verifier's design: of the 1{,}062
total violations in B$'$+E, 622 ($58.6\%$) were on fields the target
calculator's schema does not declare as required (181 of them on
\texttt{sex} alone). A schema-gated verifier that compared only
required fields would have suppressed those 622 violations without
changing the extractor. Both design choices contributed; we discuss
this jointly in \cref{sec:disc_extractor}.

\textbf{4. Reactive per-tool-call extraction \emph{partially} supported
the schema-size hypothesis.} Diagnostic markers moved as predicted ---
sex fell to 13\% of flags, correct-rate on intervention rows doubled
to 24\%, and the total token cost dropped $\sim$57\% --- but the
accuracy improvement vs upfront B$'$+E was not significant (+1.9 pp,
$p = 0.103$). Reactive remained 7.1 pp below B$'$
(\cref{sec:results_reactive}). Schema size is one contributor to
extractor unreliability but not the sole bottleneck.

\subsection{The Extractor as the Limiting Component}
\label{sec:disc_extractor}

Interwhen's mechanism is mechanically intact in our adaptation. The
verifier's comparison logic is deterministic: it does exact match for
enums, numeric match with tolerance for numbers, and no flag at all when
the extractor has no value for a field. None of that machinery can
synthesize a wrong flag on its own --- a wrong flag requires a wrong
reference. The reference comes from a single Sonnet call. The reference
\emph{is} the load-bearing component.

We can state the limit precisely. In a setting where the verifier's
reference is produced by a model $M_e$ over a field set $\mathcal{F}$,
the verifier's effect on accuracy is upper-bounded by the joint
distribution of (a) the model's input-error rate on $\mathcal{F}$, (b)
$M_e$'s extraction-error rate on $\mathcal{F}$, and (c) the asymmetric
cost of a false flag (which re-prompts and can flip correct calls)
versus a true flag (which can fix a wrong call). Under upfront
full-schema extraction, term (b) is high enough on common fields like
\verb|sex| that the false-flag rate exceeds the true-flag rate ---
hence harm rather than help.

Reactive extraction reduces term (b) for common fields, which is
visible in the flag composition shifting away from \verb|sex|. It does
not reduce term (b) enough to flip the cost balance significantly,
which is why accuracy moves only 1.9 pp.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{The extractor pipeline fails independently of
end-to-end reasoning.}\quad
A post-hoc check on the 118 harm cases (B$'$ correct $\rightarrow$
B$'$+E wrong) makes the point sharper. Sonnet's apparatus-gate run on
Condition B answered the same vignettes \emph{end-to-end} (read case,
choose calculator, call tool, return answer) at 80.4\% accuracy.
Of the 118 harm cases, Sonnet got the final answer right on \emph{101
(86\%)} despite the upfront extractor's reference (a different Sonnet
call with a different system prompt) being wrong enough on those rows
to flip Qwen3's correct tool call into a wrong one. The odds ratio of
harm given Sonnet-on-B-wrong is 2.40 --- Sonnet's overall reasoning
failures do enrich the harm population --- but they explain only a
small minority of it. The implication: the extractor's failure mode is
not ``Sonnet does not understand the case'' but ``Sonnet, asked to
populate a $\sim$500-field union schema with a different prompt, mis-pulls
common fields even when it knows the case well enough to answer the
underlying question.'' This reinforces the claim that the bottleneck
is the \emph{upfront full-schema extraction architecture} specifically,
not the model's clinical reasoning capacity.

This also sharpens an important framing concern: because the reference
itself is LLM-extracted, our experiment is partly evaluating
\emph{alignment between two LLM pipelines} (Qwen3's tool-call inputs
vs Sonnet's extracted reference) rather than the verifier mechanism in
isolation. Replacing the extractor with structured records (point (i)
below) would re-orient the same experiment toward a cleaner test of
the verifier itself.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{The verifier's comparison surface is itself a design
choice.}\quad
The verifier in this study iterates over fields the primary model
included in \texttt{input\_data}; it does not consult the target
calculator's schema to ask whether a given field is required for the
active call. A post-hoc audit finds that 622 of 1{,}062 B$'$+E
violations (58.6\%) fired on fields the calculator does not declare as
required --- the \texttt{bmi\_001} pattern (\cref{app:loop_trace}) is
the dominant case, not an outlier. The diagnosis therefore restates
precisely: the harm comes from the \emph{join} of (a) an extractor
that populates fields the active tool call does not need and (b) a
verifier whose comparison surface is the model's proposed inputs
rather than the calculator's required inputs. Either redesign would
address the \texttt{bmi\_001} class of failures; a schema-gated
verifier variant, proposed in \cref{sec:disc_followups}, would test
this directly. Reactive is one design
dimension within the reference layer; others worth exploring include
structured-EHR inputs (eliminating $M_e$ for fields the EHR carries),
retrieval-augmented extraction (grounding $M_e$ on case-text spans
rather than free-text inference), and human-in-the-loop confirmation
on flagged fields.

One refinement deserves explicit acknowledgment. ``Extractor error''
in the analysis above is broader than ``the extractor reads the wrong
value.'' \Cref{fig:loop_trace} shows a real exemplar
(\texttt{bmi\_001}) where the extractor correctly read
\texttt{sex=female}, the model proposed \texttt{sex=1} (a different
canonical form for the same fact), and the verifier flagged the
deterministic comparison because the value types differed. In this
case the verifier's comparison is mechanically correct, the extractor's
value is semantically correct, and yet the call fails. The binding
constraint is the \emph{join} of (extractor canonical form,
verifier comparison strictness, model serialization preference). Any
of these three can drive a flag that the other two cannot resolve. The
reactive condition reduces the harm partly by extracting fewer fields,
which mechanically reduces the surface where this three-way mismatch
can occur.

\subsection{Why Tool-Forcing Compounds Harm}
\label{sec:disc_toolforcing}

The verifier's firing rate scales directly with the number of tool calls.
Under E (no forcing), Qwen3 emitted a mean of 0.58 tool calls per vignette
and the verifier intervened on 2.7\% of vignettes. Under B$'$+E (forced),
mean tool calls rose to 3.67 and intervention rate rose to 13.7\%. Each
intervention is a regeneration opportunity for the model to absorb the
verifier's feedback; each feedback message carries the extracted reference.
When the reference is right, the model converges to a right call. When
the reference is wrong, the model is pulled toward a wrong call ---
because the feedback message gives the model a specific value to match.
B$'$ removes the model's option to decline a tool call when uncertain;
B$'$+E adds an unreliable corrective signal on every call the model makes.
The compounding is multiplicative, not additive: more calls $\times$
higher error injection per call.

A real exemplar of this mechanism --- vignette \texttt{bmi\_001}, drawn
directly from the result bundle --- is traced step-by-step in
\cref{app:loop_trace}. There, the upfront extractor correctly extracts
\texttt{sex=female}, the model proposes \texttt{sex=1} as part of an
otherwise correct BMI tool call, the verifier flags the type mismatch
eight times in succession, and the model exhausts its turn budget
before the calculator ever runs. Reactive extraction avoids the
failure entirely on the same vignette.

\subsection{Implications for Deploying Verifier-Guided Reasoning in Clinical AI}
\label{sec:disc_implications}

Three concrete recommendations follow from our findings.

\textit{Validate the reference pipeline independently before adopting
intermediate verification.} The verifier's correctness guarantee is no
stronger than the extractor's accuracy on the deployment's specific field
set. We recommend a held-out hand-annotated benchmark and a field-level
accuracy gate (e.g., $\geq 95\%$ on common fields) as a precondition for
production use, mirrored on the live field distribution rather than the
union of all possible MCP fields.

\textit{Prefer focused per-call extraction over upfront full-schema
extraction.} Reactive extraction did not significantly improve accuracy
in our study, but it cuts token cost substantially ($\sim$57\% fewer
total tokens, $\sim$35$\times$ fewer Sonnet input tokens) and improves
flag informativeness (correct-rate on intervention rows doubled).
Median wall-clock latency is unchanged because the sequential
per-call Sonnet requests cancel the per-call schema-size savings, so
deployments that are latency-bound rather than token-bound see no
direct benefit from this swap. On every axis except latency, reactive
appears to be a strict improvement over upfront extraction.

\textit{Consider whether the reference can be non-LLM.} Where structured
patient-record data exist (EHR fields, vital signs from devices,
laboratory results from LIS), they should be preferred over LLM
extraction. Reserve LLM extraction for fields the structured record does
not carry --- and gate it field-by-field on observed accuracy.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

We name six limitations explicitly.

\textit{Single primary model.} All conditions evaluate
Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507. A different model family or size could
show a different verifier-interaction profile. The mechanism we identify
(reference-extractor as the binding constraint) should transfer, but
the specific magnitudes will not.

\textit{Single benchmark.} medical\_calculator\_eval is one benchmark
covering deterministic calculator dispatch. Generalization to
differential-diagnosis, symptom-triage, or open-ended decision-support
tools is untested. Procedural input verification is most natural where
the tool's inputs are typed and structured; how the same mechanism
would adapt to less-structured tool surfaces is an open question.

\textit{Single extractor model.} Sonnet 4.6 is one extractor; another
model could show different per-field failure rates and thereby a
different overall verdict on the verifier. Our claim is not that
\emph{Sonnet} fails specifically, but that \emph{some} LLM extractor is
the binding constraint under upfront full-schema extraction; the
mechanism is general, the specific numbers are not.

\textit{No ground-truth-reference comparator.} We do not run a
condition in which the verifier consults a hand-annotated reference
set. Such a condition would isolate the verifier mechanism from the
extractor's fallibility entirely and is the most important comparator
the present study lacks; it is itemized in \cref{sec:disc_followups}
with its cost.

\textit{Multi-call vs schema-size confound in reactive.} The reactive
condition makes $\sim$3.6 sequential Sonnet calls per vignette versus
one for upfront, so ``smaller schema'' and ``more independent
extraction attempts'' covary in our design. The diagnostic-marker
movements in \cref{sec:results_diagnostic,sec:results_reactive} are
most consistent with the schema-size mechanism, but we cannot formally
rule out a multi-attempts contribution. The disentangling condition
(multi-call full-schema extraction) is proposed in
\cref{sec:disc_followups}.

\textit{Apparatus gate scope.} The gate validates Sonnet on Condition B
within $[0.789, 0.849]$ and passed at 80.4\%. It is a drift detector
on the harness, dataset, prompt rendering, and MCP transport. It does
not independently validate the primary Qwen3 numbers. We assume the
harness behaves identically for the primary model conditional on the
gate.

\textit{Conservative verifier silent on extractor misses.} The
verifier's ``no extractor value $\rightarrow$ no flag'' rule suppresses
false positives but means a \emph{silent} extractor (extracts nothing
for a field the model proposes) cannot trigger feedback. The verifier
is observable only when the extractor is both active and wrong. A
fully missing extraction is invisible to the firing-rate statistics ---
some fraction of vignettes may have ``silent'' verifier failures we
cannot count.

\textit{Extractor stochasticity at $T=0$.} We ran post-hoc
reproducibility checks comparing Sonnet's upfront extractions on the
same vignettes across the E and B$'$+E runs (same model, same prompt,
temperature$=0$, run on different sessions). \emph{Values} are highly
stable when both runs commit to a value --- agreement is 100\% on
\texttt{sex} (238/238 paired), 99.6\% on \texttt{age} (227/228), and
99--100\% across other high-impact fields (\texttt{weight},
\texttt{height}, \texttt{creatinine}, \texttt{diabetes}). \emph{Field
presence} is less stable: for any single field, $\sim$5--10\% of paired
vignettes have the field extracted on one side but not the other
($\sim$5\% of all rows for \texttt{sex} specifically). This is
consistent with documented non-determinism in Anthropic's API at
$T=0$. Because of the conservative no-flag rule above, presence
variance translates into \emph{missing} flags rather than wrong
flags --- a different upfront B$'$+E run would likely have slightly
fewer or more total flags but the same architectural pattern (a
single common field dominating the flag composition).

\subsection{What This Paper Does Not Claim}
\label{sec:disc_notclaim}

To preempt common misreadings, we name five claims this paper does
\emph{not} make.

\textbf{1. ``Interwhen does not work.''} It does. Interwhen's mechanism
operated mechanically as designed in every condition we ran. Our
clinical adaptation introduces an LLM-based reference layer absent
from interwhen's original math and code settings, and \emph{that layer}
is the limit. The verifier itself is sound.

\textbf{2. ``Verification of LLM outputs is unhelpful.''} We did not
test output verification as our central question. We studied procedural
\emph{input} verification specifically. Output verification, end-to-end
outcome verification, grounded retrieval, ensemble voting, and
human-in-the-loop regimes are different surfaces, and our results say
nothing about them directly. Condition D is the closest output-style
comparator in this study; it also did not significantly improve over B
but was scoped as a control, not the main object of study.

\textbf{3. ``Sonnet is a poor extractor.''} It is not. Sonnet 4.6 is
near the frontier of clinical-text extraction quality. We show that
\emph{even} Sonnet underperforms under a particular prompt regime
(upfront, $\sim$500-field flat schema) and that restructuring the
\emph{same model} into a focused regime substantially shifts the
flag-composition diagnostic. The bottleneck is the prompt architecture,
not the extractor's underlying capability.

\textbf{4. ``Tool-forcing system prompts are dangerous.''} They are
not, on their own. B$'$ alone improved accuracy by 22.3 pp over B; the
harm appears only when B$'$ is combined with an unreliable verifier
reference. Tool-forcing amplifies whatever follows it. With a reliable
reference, the amplification could just as well be positive.

\textbf{5. ``B$'$+E reactive is the right deployment configuration.''}
We report one mechanistic test. Reactive's accuracy did not significantly
beat upfront B$'$+E and remained below B$'$. The condition is cheaper
in tokens (and improves the diagnostic markers) but \emph{not} faster
in median wall-clock latency. A production decision depends on
cost-latency tradeoffs, field-level validation of the focused
extractor, and the specific clinical workflow. Treating reactive as
a recommended deployment configuration would overstate what one
comparison can show.
