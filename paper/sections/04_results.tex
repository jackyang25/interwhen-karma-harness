\subsection{Apparatus Gate}
\label{sec:results_gate}

Sonnet on Condition B reached 80.4\% accuracy ($N=1066$), within the
pre-registered band defined in \cref{sec:methods_gate}. The gate
passed ($\Delta = -1.51$ pp; the apparatus-gate JSON in the result
bundle records the exact replication). Primary contrasts below are
interpretable only because this gate held.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Primary Comparisons (Pre-Registered)}
\label{sec:results_primary}

\Cref{tab:accuracy} reports per-condition accuracy with Wilson 95\%
confidence intervals; \cref{tab:mcnemar_primary} reports the four
pre-registered McNemar contrasts at Bonferroni $\alpha = 0.0125$.

% Hero figure: forest plot of all 8 condition accuracies with Wilson CIs.
% Ordered narratively (anchors -> primary -> exploratory -> diagnostic);
% color = family. Tells the story arc in one image.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{forest_accuracy.pdf}
  \caption{Per-condition accuracy with Wilson 95\% confidence intervals
  ($N=1066$). Color encodes condition family.}
  \label{fig:forest}
\end{figure}

The headline number is null. None of the three verifier configurations
significantly improved over B. Condition E --- the interwhen-style
deterministic input verifier with upfront Sonnet extraction --- was numerically the
worst primary condition at 48.7\% (vs B's 50.4\%; McNemar
$p_{\text{Bonferroni}} = 0.157$, $\Delta = -1.7$ pp).
Conditions C (prompt-only self-verify, 49.4\%) and D (post-hoc Sonnet
verifier, 50.1\%) were also indistinguishable from B. The only
significant primary contrast was B vs A (50.4\% vs 41.6\%, $\Delta = +8.8$
pp, $p_{\text{Bonferroni}} \approx 10^{-10}$), confirming that the model
does benefit from tool access --- the calculators reach correct answers
when the model invokes them, which is the apparatus this study presupposes.

\textit{What this does not show.} The null primary result does not mean
``verification cannot help.'' It means that, in this benchmark with this
primary model and this extractor, the procedural input-verification surface
did not move accuracy at the pre-registered significance level. We turn
in \cref{sec:results_exploratory,sec:results_diagnostic} to a mechanistic
question that the null result cannot answer on its own: \emph{why didn't
the verifier help, and would a different reference pipeline change that?}

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Exploratory: The Tool-Forcing Negative Finding}
\label{sec:results_exploratory}

The B$'$ system prompt --- which instructs the model to commit to a tool
call rather than answering from prior knowledge --- moved accuracy from
50.4\% to 72.7\% (McNemar $p \approx 10^{-42}$, 270 wrong$\rightarrow$right
vs 32 right$\rightarrow$wrong). Mean tool calls per vignette rose from
0.6 to 3.7. This is a large, clear, and exploratory effect; we treat B$'$
as an exploratory \emph{baseline} for the combination conditions that
follow.

% Discordant-pairs Sankey: the visual smoking gun.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{discordant_flow.pdf}
  \caption{Paired-outcome contingency for B$'$ vs B$'$+E ($N=1066$).
  Off-diagonal cells are the discordant pairs McNemar's test operates on.}
  \label{fig:discordant_flow}
\end{figure}

Adding the E verifier on top of B$'$ moved accuracy from 72.7\% to 63.7\%
($\Delta = -9.0$ pp, McNemar $p \approx 10^{-15}$): the verifier
\emph{actively hurt}. The discordant counts are the smoking gun:
118 vignettes that were correct under B$'$ became wrong under B$'$+E,
while only 22 went the other way. Among the 146 ``intervention rows''
(vignettes where the verifier fired at least once), only 14\% ended
correct --- the verifier was disproportionately re-prompting the model
on cases it would otherwise have gotten right.

This stands in sharp contrast to E alone (without B$'$), where the
verifier fired on only 2.7\% of vignettes; under B$'$ the firing rate
rose to 13.7\%. The mechanism is straightforward: B$'$ forces tool use,
each tool call goes through the verifier, and each verifier flag
re-prompts with feedback. Exposure to the verifier's reference scales
with forced tool use.

\textit{What this does not show.} It does not show that tool-forcing
prompts are dangerous in general --- B$'$ \emph{alone} improved accuracy
by 22.3 pp. The harm is an interaction effect between forced tool use
and an \emph{unreliable reference}. The next subsection localizes the
unreliability.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Diagnostic: Verifier Flag Composition}
\label{sec:results_diagnostic}

If the verifier's mechanical comparison were the problem, flags would
distribute uniformly across the fields models actually use. They do not.
Under B$'$+E, 343 of 1062 total flags (32.3\%) fired on a single field:
\verb|sex|. The next-largest field was \verb|age| (7.6\%); the long tail
was thin. \verb|sex| is among the easiest fields to extract correctly
--- vignettes routinely state the patient's sex in the first sentence
--- so a 32\% flag share is hard to attribute to genuine model error.
It is far more parsimoniously attributed to the \emph{extractor}
producing the wrong reference when asked, in a single Sonnet call, to
populate a $\sim$500-field union schema spanning every calculator in
the MCP toolset.

% The diagnostic punchline.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{flag_composition.pdf}
  \caption{Top-10 flagged fields under upfront (left) vs reactive
  (right) extraction.}
  \label{fig:flag_composition}
\end{figure}

Two further numerical observations support this attribution.
First, the verifier's mechanical comparison is deterministic and
field-blind: it has no notion that \verb|sex| is ``easy.'' Yet
\verb|sex| dominates flags. Second, the per-field flag distribution
is dense in fields the case typically states explicitly (sex, age,
weight, height) and sparse in fields the case typically omits or
states in non-canonical units. The pattern is consistent with an
\emph{extractor} that misreads explicitly stated fields under
attention pressure from a long, dense schema --- not with a
\emph{verifier} that over-flags. The mechanism is the load on the
extractor, not the strictness of the verifier.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Follow-Up: Reactive Extraction}
\label{sec:results_reactive}

Reactive extraction --- one Sonnet call per medical\_calculator\_output
tool call, prompted only with the fields the model is about to populate
--- yielded an accuracy of 65.6\% [62.7, 68.4]. Compared to upfront
B$'$+E (63.7\%), this is a 1.9 pp numerical improvement that
\emph{does not reach significance} (McNemar $p = 0.103$, 78 wrong$\rightarrow$right
vs 58 right$\rightarrow$wrong). Compared to B$'$ (72.7\%), reactive
still hurts: $\Delta = -7.1$ pp, $p \approx 10^{-12}$.

The hypothesis going in was: \emph{if reactive recovers performance,
schema size was the bottleneck}. The accuracy result alone does not
support that. But three diagnostic markers move sharply in the
predicted direction:

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{2pt}
  \item \textbf{Sex share of flags fell from 32\% to 13\%.} This is the
        most direct test of the schema-size hypothesis: the dominant
        field flagged under upfront extraction was no longer dominant
        under focused extraction. The flag distribution flattened
        (\cref{fig:flag_composition}).
  \item \textbf{Correct-rate on intervention rows doubled,
        14\%$\rightarrow$24\%.} When the reactive verifier fired, the
        vignette was more often resolved correctly than under upfront
        firing --- the verifier's signals became more informative even
        though it fired slightly more often (164 vs 146 intervention
        rows).
  \item \textbf{Total Sonnet input-token cost dropped by an order of
        magnitude.} Mean Sonnet prompt tokens per vignette fell from
        50{,}230 (upfront) to 1{,}122 (reactive) --- the focused prompts
        are small, even summed across the $\sim$3.6 tool calls per
        vignette. Total per-vignette tokens (Sonnet + Qwen3) dropped from
        85.7K to 36.7K.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{trajectory_flow.pdf}
  \caption{Per-vignette outcome trajectories across the three conditions
  ($N=1066$), partitioned into six categories.}
  \label{fig:trajectory_flow}
\end{figure}

The trajectory view (\cref{fig:trajectory_flow}) makes the
nuance visible. The diagnostic markers shifted; accuracy did not. The
cleanest interpretation is that \emph{schema-size attention dilution is
one contributor to extractor unreliability but not the sole bottleneck}.
Other plausible contributors --- Sonnet's free-text extraction quality
on clinical narrative, verifier sensitivity on the long tail of
correctly-flagged-but-genuinely-ambiguous cases, the propagation of
feedback through subsequent regenerated tool calls --- remain
candidates the design of this study cannot separate.

We flag one design confound explicitly. Reactive performs $\sim$3.6
sequential Sonnet calls per vignette; upfront performs one. We cannot
disentangle ``smaller schema is easier'' from ``more independent
extraction attempts converge'' without a third arm
(multi-call full-schema extraction). The diagnostic-marker movements
above are most consistent with the schema-size mechanism, but we cannot
formally rule out an attempts-based mechanism contributing.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Per-Category and Stratified Effects}
\label{sec:results_category}

\Cref{fig:category_heatmap} shows per-condition accuracy across 24
calculator categories. The B$'$+E negative effect is broadly distributed
--- it appears in 17 of 24 categories and is not driven by one outlier
category. On a stratified subset of 165 vignettes where every condition
actually invoked tools (the \toolname{stratified\_tool\_subset.csv} file in the
results bundle), the same pattern holds: B = 65.5\%,
E = 50.3\%, B$'$+E = 60.6\%, B$'$+E reactive = 64.2\%. The reactive
condition is closer to B and B$'$ than to upfront B$'$+E on the
tool-invoking subset, hinting that the upfront extractor's harm is
specifically realized through tool calls --- consistent with the
input-verification mechanism --- and that focused extraction tightens
this loop.

\subsection{Cost and Latency}
\label{sec:results_cost}

Cost matters because the headline tradeoff is not just ``does the
verifier help?'' but ``at what price.'' \Cref{fig:pareto} plots the
cost--accuracy frontier; \cref{tab:cost} summarizes the key numbers.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Total tokens per vignette and median wall-clock latency by
  condition. ``API'' tokens are Sonnet calls (extractor + post-hoc
  verifier in D). ``On-GPU'' tokens are Qwen3 via vLLM.}
  \label{tab:cost}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    Condition & Total tokens & API tokens & Latency (s) \\
    \midrule
    B                 &  8{,}152 &       0 & 123 \\
    E                 & 59{,}798 & 50{,}543 &  87 \\
    B$'$              & 29{,}908 &       0 & 408 \\
    B$'$+E            & 85{,}702 & 50{,}353 & 223 \\
    B$'$+E (reactive) & 36{,}749 &  1{,}416 & 223 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two observations. First, upfront E and B$'$+E spend an enormous Sonnet
budget on extraction: $\sim$50K prompt tokens per vignette, of which
the verifier may consult only a handful of fields. Second, the reactive
condition is \emph{cheaper} than upfront B$'$+E by $\sim$57\% total tokens
($85.7K \rightarrow 36.7K$) and by $\sim$35$\times$ on Sonnet input
tokens specifically ($50.4K \rightarrow 1.4K$). Even though reactive
makes more Sonnet calls per vignette, each is small, and the larger
context-window cost of upfront extraction is eliminated. Median wall-clock
latency is essentially unchanged (223\,s for both upfront and reactive
B$'$+E): reactive trades one large Sonnet call for several smaller
\emph{sequential} ones, so the time savings on schema length cancel
against the increased call count. The reactive condition is therefore
Pareto-improved on \emph{token cost} (not on latency) without a
significant accuracy gain --- a real engineering result independent of
the headline accuracy story.

\Cref{fig:tokens} decomposes the per-condition cost into primary-model
tokens (Qwen3, on-GPU vLLM) and external API tokens (Sonnet extractor in
E/B$'$+E/reactive, Sonnet post-hoc verifier in D). The visual story is
that the Sonnet budget under upfront extraction dwarfs every other cost
component and is essentially eliminated under reactive extraction. The
primary-model token cost is similar across B$'$, B$'$+E, and B$'$+E
reactive --- forced tool use drives those tokens, not the verifier ---
so the reactive savings come entirely from the extractor architecture.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{token_decomposition.pdf}
  \caption{Per-vignette token cost decomposed into primary-model
  (Qwen3) and external API (Sonnet) tokens.}
  \label{fig:tokens}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{pareto.pdf}
  \caption{Cost--accuracy frontier. Each point is one condition.}
  \label{fig:pareto}
\end{figure}

Latency tells a different story than cost. \Cref{fig:latency} plots
the same eight conditions against median wall-clock latency, with
accuracy (left panel) and total tokens (right panel) on the y-axis.
B$'$+E and B$'$+E (reactive) sit at \emph{identical} median latency
(223\,s) despite reactive using $\sim$57\% fewer total tokens: the
schema-size savings on each Sonnet call are offset by reactive making
$\sim$3.6 sequential calls instead of one. For deployments that are
token-budget-bound, reactive is the better choice; for deployments
that are wall-clock-bound, it is not.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{latency_panels.pdf}
  \caption{Latency vs accuracy (left) and latency vs token cost (right).
  B$'$+E and B$'$+E (reactive) collapse to the same median latency,
  separating only on the cost axis.}
  \label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}
